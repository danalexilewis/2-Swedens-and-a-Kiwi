# Capital-Class Society

A working definition of one of the book’s major original formulations.

## Working definition

A capital-class society is one in which the decisive economic division is increasingly not between occupations or income groups, but between people who own appreciating assets and people who do not.

## Characteristics

1. Income covers consumption but struggles to create security.
2. Property and financial assets compound faster than wages.
3. Family wealth determines access to housing.
4. Inheritance becomes a normal requirement for adult stability.
5. Owners can borrow to acquire more ownership.
6. Non-owners pay owners for access to housing and other essentials.
7. Political policy is constrained by the need to protect asset values.
8. Risk-taking becomes concentrated among people with family support.
9. Economic mobility becomes increasingly dependent on family balance sheets.

## What this distinguishes

This lets the book distinguish a capital-class society from a society that merely has rich and poor people.

The central shift is:

> Class becomes increasingly inherited through ownership rather than earned through work.

## Compact lines

Work pays for life.

Capital determines whether you can build security.

## Safe manuscript wording

Sweden and New Zealand appear to be moving toward capital-class societies, in which family assets increasingly shape access to housing and security.

## Possible stronger wording

The two countries are replacing a working middle class with an inheriting capital class.
