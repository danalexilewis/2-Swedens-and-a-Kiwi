# Key Concepts

Short definitions for terms the book uses repeatedly.

## Shared Failure

Political and economic management that protects incumbent asset values and short-term stability without adequately managing long-term distributional consequences. See `SHARED-FAILURE.md`.

## Capital-class society

A society in which the decisive economic division is increasingly between people who own appreciating assets and people who do not. Class becomes increasingly inherited through ownership rather than earned through work. See `CAPITAL-CLASS-SOCIETY.md`.

## Unconstrained intergenerational inequality

Not “literally untaxed.” The system lacks strong enough mechanisms to stop wealth advantages from compounding, concentrating, and passing between generations faster than work can create equivalent security.

## Cost of economic membership

The rising price of the assets and conditions required to participate in a secure adult economic life — especially housing — as distinct from ordinary consumer inflation.

## Formal equality / unequal capacity

Rules or tax mechanisms that are formally available to everyone, but whose greatest benefits flow to people who already have capital.

## Inheritance contract

The emerging pattern in which the social contract that once promised security through work is replaced by dependence on family transfers and inheritance for adult stability.

## Skill and structural tailwind

When discussing identifiable successful people: business skill and extraordinary monetary/property-market conditions can coexist. Avoid “pure luck” and avoid inventing motives.

## Argument against denial

The book’s present-tense purpose: admit where past choices have brought us, even if those choices were understandable at the time.

## Land

The site and location itself — not the buildings or other improvements on it. In this book’s Georgist-adjacent usage, land is the scarce, non-produced location whose value rises with community activity, infrastructure, and planning.

## Improvements

Buildings, renovations, privately built infrastructure, and other produced capital attached to a site. Distinguishing improvements from land is the point of a land-value tax.

## Land rent

The return attributable to control of a location, as distinct from the return to buildings or other produced capital. Population growth, public investment, and nearby activity can raise land rent without the owner creating equivalent value.

## Economic rent

Income from control of a scarce resource or privilege beyond what is needed to bring that resource into use — including, but not limited to, land rent. Broader than “rent” as a monthly housing payment.

## Productive capital

Produced means of production and useful assets created by investment, work, and entrepreneurship — buildings, equipment, firms, housing services — as distinct from unreclaimed gains in scarce land value.

## Rentier income

Income (or wealth gain) from owning scarce claims — especially land and other non-produced advantages — rather than from creating additional goods, services, or productive capacity.

## Land-value tax

A recurring tax on the value of land excluding buildings and other improvements. Not the same as a vacant-land tax. Hypothetical rates in working notes are illustrative, not a finished proposal.

## Capital-value property tax

A tax on total property value (land plus buildings/improvements). Improving the building can increase the tax base — the contrast that makes pure land-value taxation conceptually attractive.

## Tax incidence

Who ultimately bears the economic burden of a tax after prices, wages, and asset values adjust — not merely who writes the cheque.

## Tax capitalization

When a recurring tax (or its absence) is reflected in the upfront price of an asset. A new land-value tax can lower the selling price of land; existing owners at introduction often take the initial loss.

## Land-value capture

Policy tools that recover some of the increase in land value created by public infrastructure or administrative actions such as rezoning — so that socially created location value is not left entirely with existing owners.

## Georgism

The tradition stemming from Henry George’s argument that land rents, rather than work and produced capital, should bear more of the tax burden. Useful as a framework for Shared Failure; the book does not assume George’s full nineteenth-century single-tax programme applies unchanged.

## Henry George theorem

Formal result associated with Arnott and Stiglitz: under stated conditions, the value of land rents relates to the cost of public goods. It supports the idea that public investment is reflected in land rents; it does not prove every project pays for itself through land value alone.
