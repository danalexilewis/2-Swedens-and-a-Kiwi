# Voice

## Principles

- Plain language.
- Opinionated.
- Anger preserved where it is earned.
- Facts distinguished from judgments.
- Sweden and New Zealand compared without being collapsed into one story.
- Lived scenes open arguments; they do not replace them.
- Do not weaken strong opinions into neutral corporate prose.
- Do not invent transitions, details, motives, or evidence.

## Chapter grammar

Most chapters should follow this movement:

1. A lived scene
2. The shared failure
3. The New Zealand mechanism
4. The Swedish mechanism
5. The common result
6. Who benefited
7. Who carried the cost
8. The strongest counterargument
9. Your conclusion
10. What must now be faced

This allows the book to be opinionated without becoming repetitive. The reader sees how the judgment was reached.

## Claim types in prose

| Type | How it should read |
|------|--------------------|
| Fact | Stated plainly, with a reference when it is a load-bearing number, date, rule, or institutional claim |
| Personal experience | Owned as experience (“Had we possessed…”) |
| Interpretation | Clearly a judgment (“The system is profoundly unfair”) |
| Causal argument | Reasoning made visible; several supporting pieces |
| Prediction | Identified as a prediction (“If these trends continue…”) |
| Proposal | Framed as what should happen next |

## What not to do

- Do not write as if every sentence needs a citation.
- Do not write as if official bodies have certified your judgments.
- Do not attribute motives to identifiable people.
- Do not claim “pure luck” where skill and structural advantage coexist.
- Do not turn the archive into the product.
