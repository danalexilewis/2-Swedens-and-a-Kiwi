## Title

Two Swedens and a Kiwi

## Purpose

To argue that Sweden and New Zealand are becoming capital-class societies, and that this is the result of a shared political and economic management failure: allowing wealth advantages to compound and transfer between generations without meaningful limits or sufficient counterweights.

## Reader promise

You will get a clear comparative argument in plain language, grounded in lived experience, supported by enough evidence to be credible, and honest about the difference between fact, interpretation, causal claim, and prediction.

You will not get an exposé, an academic thesis, a forensic trial of every past decision, or a chronological autobiography.

## Central thesis

Sweden and New Zealand are becoming capital-class societies: countries where a person’s security and opportunities increasingly depend less on what they earn and contribute, and more on what they and their family already own.

## Shared Failure theme

Political and economic management in both countries has allowed ownership advantages to compound across generations without meaningful limits or sufficient counterweights.

The result is unconstrained intergenerational inequality.

“Unconstrained” does not mean literally untaxed or entirely unregulated. It means the system does not contain strong enough mechanisms to stop wealth advantages from compounding, concentrating, and passing between generations faster than work can create equivalent security.

## What the book is

- An opinionated comparative economics book
- Written in plain language
- Supported by lived experience and references
- An argument against denial in the present
- Fair about difficult tradeoffs in the past

## What the book is not

- An exposé
- An academic thesis
- A work of investigative journalism
- A chronological autobiography
- A trial of every past decision as if it were obviously wrong at the time
- Proof submitted for a courtroom

## Author position

Some emergency decisions may have been reasonable. Some tradeoffs were genuinely difficult. But once the distributional effects became clear, both countries repeatedly failed to correct course.

The book is not primarily a trial of the past. It is an argument against denial in the present.

## Tone

Opinionated. Plain-spoken. Angry where anger is warranted. Careful where claims involve numbers, institutions, or identifiable people. Never neutralized into corporate prose.

## Main outcomes being examined

- The erosion or death of the broadly accessible home-owning middle class
- Housing security becoming dependent on inheritance
- Reduced entrepreneurial risk-taking
- Declining productivity
- Delayed or reduced family formation
- Youth alienation
- Brain drain
- Lower trust in political institutions
- The emergence of capital-class societies

## Destination

The death of the broadly accessible home-owning middle class and the emergence of a society divided increasingly by capital ownership.

That does not necessarily mean the disappearance of all middle-income professionals. It means income alone becomes insufficient to secure what a middle-class income previously promised:

- A stable home
- Room for children
- Reasonable independence
- The ability to take professional or entrepreneurial risks
- A secure retirement
- A belief that the next generation can establish itself

**Work pays for life. Capital determines whether you can build security.**
