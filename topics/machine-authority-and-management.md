# Topic: Machine Authority and Management

## Shared Failure

A branch of Shared Failure that is not only about asset inequality, but about declining confidence in the humans and institutions entrusted to exercise power over us. People may become attracted to machine authority not because machines are wonderful, but because their experience of human authority has become so poor.

*(interpretation)*

See also: `politics-and-management`, `youth-and-work`, `institutions-worth-keeping`, `reflection-and-execution`.

## Why It Matters

Most of the literature asks either “Will algorithmic management exploit workers?” or “Can AI make management and government more efficient?” The distinctive question here is psychological:

> Why does machine authority sometimes feel attractive in the first place?

Answer in force: because human authority has become intolerable to a lot of people.

That opens a progression worth keeping intact:

Bad human management → workers value autonomy → platforms remove the visible manager → algorithms quietly recreate managerial control → workers gain some freedom but lose security and recourse → generative AI offers a patient personalized helper → the same idea becomes imaginable at organizational and even political scale.

Warning through the whole arc:

> Removing the human manager doesn't remove power. It may merely make power harder to see.

*(interpretation / causal-argument)*

## Author position

Not a simple “AI boss good” or “AI boss bad” stance.

Working tension to preserve:

- **If people would rather be managed by a machine than by another person, the interesting question isn't what happened to AI. It's what happened to management.**
- **The machine may remove humiliation without removing exploitation.**

Likely organisational direction (compatible with how the book thinks about institutions): use machines for consistency, patience, memory, and routine coordination; retain humans for judgment, empathy, and accountability.

On gig / platform work: do not dismiss the emancipatory idea. Ask why we cannot retain **“work when your capacity allows”** without also requiring **“carry all the economic risk yourself.”**

*(interpretation / proposal-adjacent)*

## Important Differences

Labour-law institutions, union density, and platform regulation differ. Shared pattern candidate: declining trust in visible human managers and remote institutions, plus rising comfort with algorithmic or AI-mediated authority — without that comfort proving machines are fair, accountable, or non-exploitative.

Do not collapse NZ and Sweden into one “AI boss” story. Compare the institutional surround: who can negotiate the terms of algorithmic power.

## Common Mechanism

Private government at work (human managers with little accountability) → desire for autonomy and escape from humiliation → platforms and algorithms remove the visible boss while embedding rules in software, pricing, ratings, nudges, and information asymmetries → some real gains in flexibility and perceived fairness → muted emotional response to machine judgment can make exploitation easier → same attraction starts scaling toward organisational and political “machine authority.”

*(causal-argument — to be supported, not treated as settled law)*

## Who Benefits?

Platform capital and firms that can set rules in code while advertising entrepreneurship; managers who offload contested judgments onto “the system”; political actors who can gesture at AI without rebuilding accountable institutions.

*(interpretation)*

## Who Carries the Cost?

Workers under algorithmic control without recourse; people who gain schedule flexibility but lose income security, benefits, and voice; citizens offered machine participation without clear accountability; anyone for whom “fairer than a bad boss” becomes the ceiling rather than a floor.

*(interpretation)*

## Strongest Counterargument

People still prefer humans when empathy matters; surveys of AI government are often overread; algorithms can be biased and opaque; unions and labour law already exist as answers; wanting fewer politicians is not the same as wanting AI rulers; “I’d rather deal with the machine” may reflect temporary novelty or specific bad bosses, not a civilisational shift.

*(interpretation — steelman)*

## My Response

The claim is not that people already want machine dictatorship, or that algorithms are kinder. It is that poor human authority makes machine authority emotionally intelligible — and that removing humiliation is not the same as removing exploitation or restoring accountable power. Sharpened political line:

> We haven't reached the point where people straightforwardly want machines to govern them. We've reached the point where giving machines a meaningful role in government no longer sounds absurd.

*(interpretation)*
