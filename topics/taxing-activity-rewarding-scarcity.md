# Topic: Taxing Activity, Rewarding Scarcity

## Shared Failure

Both countries tax productive activity more consistently than they reclaim the land-value gains that society collectively creates. That structural asymmetry rewards ownership of scarce locations over work, construction, and entrepreneurship — one mechanism through which Shared Failure becomes intergenerational inequality.

*(interpretation / causal-argument)*

See also: `tax-preferences`, `housing-and-belonging`, `productivity-and-risk`, `inheritance-and-generations`.

## Why It Matters

The distinction is not simply rich versus poor. It is productive activity versus the passive capture of scarcity value.

A person who finances, builds, manages, and maintains apartments is doing something economically different from someone who owns valuable land, leaves it underused, and waits for the surrounding community to make it more valuable. Those activities should not automatically receive the same moral or tax treatment.

This is a conceptual framework and research direction — not yet a complete policy proposal or final chapter.

*(interpretation)*

## Author position

Not against: business, developers, landlords as a category, private wealth, profiting from useful activity, or people building and managing housing.

Against: a tax and economic system that can reward ownership of scarce land more reliably than it rewards work, construction, entrepreneurship, productive investment, wage growth, and creating additional housing.

Clarifying line: I am not against capital. I am against a system that rewards controlling scarcity more reliably than creating abundance.

Political framing that fits this position: don’t just tax capital more — change what we tax. More on scarce land, economic rents, and very large inherited advantages; less on wages, productive investment, building, and entrepreneurship. A blanket CGT can blur founder/productive-capital gains with unreclaimed location windfalls; land tax targets the latter more cleanly.

Respect the work someone put into their house (mortgage, renovation, interest-rate risk, maintenance, deferred consumption) while still asking: how much of the increase did they actually create?

## Important Differences

Instruments differ (NZ rates and property-sale rules vs Swedish property fees, ISK, and housing-gain treatment). Do not collapse them into one policy. Compare the pattern: productive activity is easier to observe and tax than unreclaimed social land rent.

Australia supplies useful mainstream case material (Henry Review; ACT stamp-duty → land-based rates reform) without making Australia the book’s third country.

## Common Mechanism

Society creates infrastructure and opportunity → desirable locations rise in value → existing owners gain equity → equity provides collateral → collateral provides access to more credit → owners acquire more assets → the advantage passes to the next generation.

The renter helps fund the society that makes the location desirable. The owner receives the land appreciation.

Working productivity proposition (research needed): a tax system that rewards land appreciation more reliably than business investment encourages capital to flow toward existing property rather than productive enterprise.

*(causal-argument — to be supported)*

## Broad land-value tax vs vacant-land tax

**Vacant-land tax:** special tax only when land is deemed empty or underused.

**Broad land-value tax:** recurring tax on land value regardless of vacant, rented, owner-occupied, or developed use — without the state judging “sufficient” use of each parcel.

Do not confuse critiques of narrow vacancy taxes with critiques of broad LVT.

## Illustrative worked examples (hypothetical)

Assume a **hypothetical** annual land-value tax of 1%. Figures are illustrative, **not** a proposed final rate.

**Owner A — underused prime land:** land NZ$10m, building NZ$500k, one home or none → annual LVT NZ$100,000.

**Owner B — productive developer:** land NZ$2m, buildings NZ$8m, 30 apartments → annual LVT NZ$20,000.

The developer is not taxed more merely for creating NZ$8m of buildings. The scarce-location owner pays more for controlling more land value.

**Same land value, different intensity:** three houses on NZ$2.1m land (NZ$21,000 LVT ≈ NZ$7,000/home) vs 12 apartments on the same NZ$2.1m land (same NZ$21,000 ≈ NZ$1,750/home). Intensive use is not a special exemption; it spreads land cost across more homes.

If LVT replaces or reduces income, company, payroll, stamp duty, or improvement taxes, some efficient housing providers could pay less total tax. That is not necessarily a failure: the goal is not to make every landlord poorer, but to move burden from productive activity toward private capture of scarce land value.

## Who Benefits?

Existing owners of appreciating locations; families who can compound land equity into credit and further assets; political coalitions that protect asset values without reclaiming social land rent.

*(interpretation)*

## Who Carries the Cost?

Workers and builders whose activity is repeatedly taxed; renters who help fund the city without capturing land appreciation; would-be founders when capital prefers property over productive enterprise; younger cohorts facing membership priced through ownership.

## Strongest Counterargument

Rates already tax property; some sale gains are taxed; stamp duty and transaction taxes exist; a broad LVT hits cash-poor land-rich owners, mortgages, and banks; valuation is hard; exemptions can shift burden onto renters and commercial property; New Zealand evidence does not prove a construction boom from land-based rating; LVT alone does not fix housing affordability or inheritance.

*(interpretation — steelman)*

## My Response

The argument is not: developers good, landlords bad. It is not: introduce one tax and housing is fixed.

It is: our tax system is much better at observing and taxing productive activity than at reclaiming the value society collectively creates in land. That creates a structural advantage for ownership over production.

Economic theory suggests taxing land rather than improvements should reduce the penalty on building. Do not yet say NZ evidence proves a large construction boom. Transition (deferral, phase-in, valuation) must be acknowledged. LVT is one part of a broader distinction between productive wealth and rent extraction — not a complete answer to Shared Failure.

*(interpretation)*
