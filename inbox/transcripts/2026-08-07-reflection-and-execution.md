Yes — **this feels like a much sharper formulation of the book's central mechanism**.

There are really two separate capabilities here:

**1. A society's capacity to act on itself.**  
Can it deliberately create institutions, coordinate people, build infrastructure, change incentives, and make a new system stick?

**2. A society's capacity to observe itself.**  
Can it step outside its own assumptions, describe what it is doing, notice when the rules are producing strange outcomes, and seriously entertain alternatives?

Your Sweden/New Zealand contrast becomes fascinating when you put it on those two axes.

### Sweden: strong at action, weaker at reflection

Sweden has an extraordinary ability to institutionalize things.

Once something becomes accepted as the way to organize society, Sweden can:
- coordinate around it;
- build institutions around it;
- standardize it;
- fund it;
- professionalize it;
- make it work nationally.

That's an enormous strength.

But the corresponding weakness might be that **a highly coherent system becomes difficult to see from inside itself**.

If almost everybody encounters the same institutions, expectations, social norms and life trajectory, they become *reality*, rather than one possible configuration of reality.

The question becomes less:

> "Is this system still producing what we want?"

and more:

> "Why are some people failing to succeed within the system?"

That's exactly your point about young Swedes potentially experiencing structural difficulty as **personal failure**.

---

### New Zealand: strong at reflection, weaker at action

New Zealand has almost the inverse characteristic.

There's a cultural willingness to say:

> "Well, that's stupid. Why do we do it that way?"

There's experimentation, irreverence, improvisation, individualism and relatively little reverence for grand institutions.

That's enormously useful for questioning systems.

But New Zealand often seems much less capable of going from:

> "We should do this differently"

to:

> **"We have redesigned the system nationally and will now sustain that change for thirty years."**

Things become pilots.

Initiatives.

Reviews.

Strategies.

Local experiments.

Then governments change, priorities change, funding disappears and institutional memory evaporates.

So perhaps:

> **Sweden knows how to make systems durable. New Zealand knows how to make systems questionable.**

And a healthy society needs both.

That is an extremely good contrast for your book.

---

## Which makes the shared failure much more important

Because now the fact that Sweden and New Zealand are experiencing similar economic symptoms isn't merely an interesting comparison.

It's a **diagnostic technique**.

Imagine two completely different software systems.

Different programming languages.  
Different architectures.  
Different developers.  
Different histories.

But they're both producing the same error.

That immediately tells you something.

You stop looking only at the implementation.

You start asking:

> **What dependency do these two systems share?**

That's what Sweden and New Zealand give you.

They have very different:
- cultures;
- welfare states;
- labor traditions;
- geography;
- industrial histories;
- tax systems;
- political histories;
- attitudes toward the state.

Yet you can find eerily familiar contemporary problems around housing, generational wealth, public-system strain, productivity, economic insecurity and people's sense that the social contract isn't delivering.

**The sameness becomes evidence.**

Not proof of any particular cause. But a clue that perhaps we're looking in the wrong place when we explain everything through national politics.

Maybe some of the important causes exist **one level above the nation-state**.

Global capital.

Financialization.

Asset inflation.

Monetary regimes.

International tax competition.

Technology.

Global supply chains.

Energy.

Demographic transitions.

The structure of modern capitalism itself.

That's where **Shared Failure** becomes more than a catchy theme.

It's almost a research method:

> **When two substantially different systems converge on the same failure state, investigate their shared structural constraints.**

---

## And then you get cross-pollination

This is the really optimistic part.

Because difference becomes an asset.

If Sweden and New Zealand were nearly identical, there wouldn't be much to learn.

But because their strengths and weaknesses are almost orthogonal, you can ask:

### What can Sweden transpose from New Zealand?

Not "copy New Zealand policy."

Something subtler:

**How do you create permission to deviate?**

How do you give individuals more legitimate ways of constructing a good life?

How do you normalize questioning institutions?

How do you make experimentation socially safe?

How do you retain collective solidarity without requiring cultural uniformity?

Sweden doesn't need to become New Zealand.

It might just need some **New Zealand-ish properties**.

---

### And what can New Zealand transpose from Sweden?

Again, not:

> Let's copy Swedish taxes.

Instead:

**How do you develop institutional muscle?**

How do you coordinate around a twenty-year national objective?

How do government, industry, universities and labor build capabilities together?

How do you turn political intention into persistent infrastructure?

How do you create institutions that survive governments?

New Zealand doesn't need to become Sweden.

It might need some **Swedish properties**.

---

And this gives you an important distinction:

> **Don't transpose institutions. Transpose capabilities.**

That's systems thinking.

Taking some Swedish policy and dropping it into New Zealand could easily fail, because that policy is sitting inside an ecosystem of cultural norms, institutions, tax structures, histories and expectations.

Instead you ask:

> What *function* does this Swedish institution perform?

Then:

> What would perform that same function within New Zealand's system?

And vice versa.

That's a far more sophisticated way of learning between countries than the usual:

**"Sweden does X, therefore New Zealand should do X."**

---

There's also a philosophical idea adjacent to what you're remembering: **reflexivity** — the capacity of a system or actor to observe and respond to itself — and, in systems/cybernetics traditions, the move toward observing not just a system but **how the system observes itself**.

You don't need to make the book philosophically heavy.

But the idea could quietly sit underneath the whole thing:

> **A mature society should be capable of describing the system it lives inside.**

Because if it cannot describe it, it cannot deliberately change it.

And if it can describe it endlessly but cannot act, that isn't sufficient either.

So perhaps the actual pairing isn't Sweden vs New Zealand at all.

It's:

**Reflection ↔ Execution**

New Zealand needs stronger execution.

Sweden needs stronger reflection.

And **Two Swedens and a Kiwi** uses their shared economic failure as the place where those two capabilities can meet.

There is even a wonderfully simple systems-thinking punchline buried in that:

> **Sweden needs New Zealand to help it ask, "Why?"  
> New Zealand needs Sweden to help it answer, "Okay, so what are we going to build instead?"**

That could carry a surprising amount of the book.
