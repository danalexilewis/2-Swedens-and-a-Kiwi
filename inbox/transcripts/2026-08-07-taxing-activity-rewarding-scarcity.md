Cursor Handover — Taxing Activity, Rewarding Scarcity

Context

This note captures a developing argument for Two Swedens and a Kiwi.

The wider book theme remains Shared Failure: Sweden and New Zealand have followed different political and institutional paths but have produced many of the same outcomes, including unaffordable housing, intergenerational inequality, weakened productivity, and growing dependence on inherited capital.

This material introduces Georgism and land-value taxation as a possible way to explain one part of that failure.

It should not yet be treated as a complete policy proposal or final chapter. It is a conceptual framework, a set of working arguments, and a research direction.

Author’s current position

I am not against:

* Business
* Developers
* Landlords as a category
* Private wealth
* Profiting from useful activity
* People building and managing housing

I am against a tax and economic system that can reward ownership of scarce land more reliably than it rewards:

* Work
* Construction
* Entrepreneurship
* Productive investment
* Wage growth
* Creating additional housing

The distinction I am trying to make is not simply:

Rich versus poor.

It is:

Productive activity versus the passive capture of scarcity value.

A person who finances, builds, manages, and maintains apartments is doing something economically different from someone who owns valuable land, leaves it underused, and waits for the surrounding community to make it more valuable.

Those activities should not automatically receive the same moral or tax treatment.

Core reasoning

My initial observation was:

The person building apartments is paying wages, buying goods, paying contractors, and generating taxable economic activity. Workers pay income tax. They then spend their post-tax income and pay GST. The developer may pay tax on company profits and may have GST obligations. Meanwhile, a person can hold land, build nothing, employ almost nobody, and become wealthier as everyone around them makes the location more valuable.

This is directionally right, but it needs careful wording.

The productive chain generates many taxable flows:

Investment
    ↓
Architects and engineers
    ↓
Materials and suppliers
    ↓
Construction workers
    ↓
Wages and business income
    ↓
Completed housing
    ↓
Sale, operation, or rental

Income earned by individuals and businesses enters the income-tax system. New Zealand GST is charged at 15% on most taxable supplies, although GST is designed as a tax on final consumption: registered businesses can generally claim credits for GST paid on business inputs, so it should not be described as a full 15% tax accumulating at every production stage. Property developers may have GST obligations when property sales form part of a taxable activity. Long-term residential accommodation is generally GST-exempt.  

The comparison is therefore not:

Every builder pays GST at every stage while a landowner pays literally nothing.

It is:

Productive activity creates visible and repeatedly measured taxable income and consumption. The increase in land value can remain largely unrealized and may not face an equivalent recurring tax merely because the location has become more valuable.

A landowner still pays:

* Council rates
* Financing costs
* Maintenance and insurance where relevant
* Transaction costs
* Tax on rental income where applicable
* Tax on a sale when New Zealand’s intention, bright-line, dealer, developer, or related land-sale rules apply

New Zealand’s rules do tax some property-sale profits, including cases involving an intention to resell and sales within the applicable bright-line period. The argument must therefore never say that every property gain is untaxed.  

The sharper argument is:

New Zealand does not generally impose an annual tax on the increase in the unimproved value of land. This allows an owner to receive a large balance-sheet gain without first creating a correspondingly large taxable economic flow.

Mark that as a claim requiring final tax-policy verification before publication.

The Georgist insight

Henry George’s central question in Progress and Poverty was why material progress could coexist with continued poverty and insecurity. His answer focused heavily on land rents: population growth, infrastructure, business activity, and technological progress make locations more valuable, allowing landowners to capture an increasing share of the benefit.  

A land-value tax charges the owner according to the value of the land itself, excluding the building and other improvements.

The purpose is to distinguish between:

Value the owner creates

* Buildings
* Renovations
* Infrastructure built privately
* Productive businesses
* Housing services
* Maintenance and management

Value created around the owner

* Population growth
* Public transport
* Schools
* Hospitals
* Roads
* Parks
* Safety
* Planning changes
* Nearby businesses
* The wider community’s productivity and desirability

The OECD describes land-value capture as a way for governments to recover some of the increase in land value generated by infrastructure and administrative actions such as rezoning.  

Worked example

Assume a hypothetical annual land-value tax of 1%.

These figures are illustrative, not a proposed final rate.

Owner A — underused prime land

* Land value: NZ$10 million
* Building value: NZ$500,000
* Homes provided: one, or none
* Annual land-value tax: NZ$100,000

Owner B — productive developer

* Land value: NZ$2 million
* Buildings and improvements: NZ$8 million
* Homes provided: 30 apartments
* Annual land-value tax: NZ$20,000

The developer is not taxed more merely because they created NZ$8 million of additional buildings.

The owner of the scarce NZ$10 million location pays more because they control much more land value.

That is intentional.

The tax says:

We will not punish you for putting useful things on land. We will charge you for the value of the scarce location you exclusively control.

The owner of three homes

The number of homes someone owns is not necessarily the best Georgist measure.

Imagine a person owns three rental houses:

Property	Land value	Building value
House 1	NZ$700,000	NZ$300,000
House 2	NZ$700,000	NZ$300,000
House 3	NZ$700,000	NZ$300,000
Total	NZ$2.1 million	NZ$900,000

At 1%, the annual land-value tax is:

NZ$21,000

Now imagine another owner has 12 apartments on one site with the same NZ$2.1 million underlying land value.

Their total land-value tax might also be:

NZ$21,000

The first owner’s land tax is effectively NZ$7,000 per home.

The apartment owner’s land tax is effectively NZ$1,750 per home.

The apartment owner has not received a special exemption. They have used the scarce land more intensively.

This is one of the clearest Georgist incentives:

Do not tax the production of more homes as heavily as the control of valuable land.

Could a landlord pay less total tax?

Possibly.

It depends on what other taxes the land-value tax replaces or reduces.

If a country introduced land-value taxation while reducing:

* Income tax
* Company tax
* Payroll taxes
* Stamp duty
* Taxes on improvements

some developers and landlords who provide housing efficiently could pay less total tax.

That is not necessarily a failure of Georgism.

The goal is not:

Make every landlord poorer.

The goal is:

Move the tax burden away from productive activity and toward the private capture of scarce land value.

A landlord who builds, maintains, and manages 100 apartments may deserve more favorable treatment than someone holding a single empty site worth the same amount.

That is politically counterintuitive but economically coherent.

Why land rather than the entire property?

A tax on total property value includes both:

* The land
* The building

That means constructing or improving a building can increase the tax base.

A pure land-value tax does not tax the improvement.

New Zealand research by Andrew Coleman and Arthur Grimes describes land taxation as relatively efficient because land is immobile, while taxing total capital value also taxes improvements.  

Research by Arthur Grimes, Andrew Aitken, and Suzi Kerr also found that land taxes were more progressive than capital-value taxes within the New Zealand local-government systems they examined.  

However, the empirical evidence should not be overstated. A New Zealand quasi-natural experiment studied by Norman Gemmell and Arthur Grimes found little short-term evidence of effects on new building development after Auckland councils changed rating systems, although there was stronger evidence of effects on alterations.  

The manuscript should therefore say:

Economic theory suggests that taxing land rather than improvements should reduce the penalty on building.

It should not yet say:

New Zealand evidence proves that a land-value tax will cause a large construction boom.

The public-investment problem

Suppose the government builds a railway station near privately owned land.

The station is paid for through:

* Income taxes
* GST
* Government borrowing
* Rates
* Public investment

The location becomes more useful.

The nearby land rises from NZ$5 million to NZ$7 million.

The private owner receives a NZ$2 million increase in land value.

The public receives the station, but the landowner receives much of the immediately capitalized financial benefit.

The key question is:

Why do we tax the workers who build the railway more consistently than we capture the increase in land value the railway creates?

Richard Arnott and Joseph Stiglitz formalized a relationship between public expenditure and aggregate land rents in what became known as the Henry George theorem. The theorem does not establish that every public project exactly pays for itself through land value, but it provides a formal basis for the idea that public goods are reflected in land rents.  

Relationship to Shared Failure

This fits Two Swedens and a Kiwi because the book’s larger argument is that Sweden and New Zealand have spent decades creating formally universal systems whose greatest absolute benefits go to those already holding capital.

Land is a particularly clear example:

Society creates infrastructure and opportunity
                ↓
Desirable locations become more valuable
                ↓
Existing owners gain equity
                ↓
Equity provides collateral
                ↓
Collateral provides access to more credit
                ↓
Owners acquire more assets
                ↓
The advantage passes to the next generation

The renter helps fund the society that makes the location desirable.

The owner receives the land appreciation.

That is one mechanism through which Shared Failure becomes intergenerational inequality.

Connection to productivity

The working proposition is:

A tax system that rewards land appreciation more reliably than business investment encourages capital to flow toward existing property rather than productive enterprise.

That may help explain why a country can simultaneously complain about:

* Weak productivity
* Low business investment
* Housing shortages
* High household debt
* Poor wage growth

while continuing to make property an unusually attractive vehicle for accumulating family wealth.

This is a causal argument requiring evidence. Do not state it as settled fact until the relationship between tax treatment, lending, capital allocation, and business investment has been researched.

Important distinction: land banking versus a broad land-value tax

Do not confuse:

A vacant-land tax

A special tax applied only when land is considered empty or underused.

A broad land-value tax

A recurring tax on land value regardless of whether the site is vacant, rented, owner-occupied, or developed.

A New Zealand Productivity Commission working paper argued that narrowly targeted vacant-land taxes could reduce developers’ inventories of land, weaken their ability to respond to future demand, and act partly as a tax on the development pipeline. That is an important challenge to simplistic “tax all vacant land immediately” arguments.  

A broad land-value tax is different because it does not require the state to decide whether each parcel is being used sufficiently well.

It says:

This is the value of the location. The owner pays according to that value.

Difficult transition questions

A land-value tax is theoretically simple but politically difficult.

Existing owners take the initial loss

Future buyers will account for the recurring tax when deciding how much they can pay.

That can reduce the capitalized selling price of land.

The person most affected is therefore often the person who owns land when the reform is introduced.

This is not necessarily unjust, but it must be acknowledged.

Cash-poor, land-rich owners

An older person may own valuable urban land but have little current income.

Possible responses include:

* Deferring the tax until sale
* Deferring it until death
* Registering the deferred amount against the property
* Phasing the system in slowly
* Charging a low government interest rate
* Providing carefully designed hardship rules

The purpose should be to capture land value over time, not to create arbitrary forced sales.

Mortgage and banking effects

If land prices fall, some highly leveraged owners could face negative equity.

Banks’ mortgage security would also be affected.

Transition must therefore be gradual and modeled carefully.

Valuation

The government must distinguish:

* Land value
* Building value
* Development potential
* Zoning effects
* Site-specific constraints

New Zealand already has land-value and capital-value rating experience, but valuation quality and appeal rights remain central implementation questions.

It does not solve every inequality

Land-value taxation does not by itself address:

* ISK concentration in Sweden
* Financial portfolios
* Company ownership
* Large inheritances
* Monopoly rents
* Unequal wages
* Pension inequality

It is one part of a broader distinction between productive wealth and rent extraction.

Australian implementation evidence

Australia provides useful mainstream and practical material.

The Australia’s Future Tax System Review, chaired by Ken Henry, recommended a broad land-tax base, possible increasing marginal rates based on land value per square meter, and a long-term move toward including most land. It also proposed replacing stamp duties with more efficient taxes such as land tax and emphasized the need for transitional rules.  

The Australian Capital Territory began a 20-year reform in 2012 that progressively reduced stamp duty and insurance duty while increasing reliance on a broader general-rates system based on land. The program was designed as a tax-mix change rather than a simple increase in the overall burden.  

This is an important case study because it shows that the practical policy is often not:

Add a huge new tax tomorrow.

It is:

Gradually replace taxes on transactions and productive activity with recurring taxation of land.

Claim-ledger entries

Claim 1

Statement: New Zealand’s tax system places clear recurring taxes on income and consumption while imposing no equivalent general recurring tax on increases in unimproved land value.

Type: Interpretation based on tax-system design.

Status: Needs precise verification and wording.

Counterargument: Rates already tax property, and some property-sale gains are taxable.

Safer wording: New Zealand taxes income and consumption broadly, while increases in land value are generally taxed less consistently and often only when particular sale rules apply.

Claim 2

Statement: Taxing total property value can discourage improvements more than taxing land value alone.

Type: Economic claim.

Status: Strong theoretical support; mixed empirical magnitude.

Sources: Coleman and Grimes; Gemmell and Grimes; Henry Review.

Claim 3

Statement: Public infrastructure and planning decisions create land-value gains that are largely captured by existing owners.

Type: Economic mechanism.

Status: Strong general support; specific examples require evidence.

Sources: OECD land-value capture work; Arnott and Stiglitz.

Claim 4

Statement: Preferential treatment of land and housing directs capital away from productive business investment.

Type: Causal argument.

Status: Research needed.

Evidence needed:

* Bank lending allocation
* Household portfolios
* Business investment trends
* Relative after-tax returns
* Mortgage lending compared with productive lending
* New Zealand and Swedish comparisons

Claim 5

Statement: A land-value tax could reduce the tax penalty on constructing additional housing.

Type: Policy prediction.

Status: Theoretically plausible; practical result depends on rate, replacement taxes, planning, finance, and transition.

Claim 6

Statement: A broad land-value tax is different from a targeted vacant-land tax.

Type: Definition.

Status: Strong.

Importance: Do not use research criticizing narrow vacancy taxes as though it automatically disproves broad land-value taxation.

Open research questions

1. How much of New Zealand’s current tax revenue comes from labor income, company income, consumption, rates, and property-related gains?
2. How are land-value rates currently used across New Zealand councils?
3. What changed when Auckland moved from several rating systems toward capital-value rating?
4. Does capital-value rating materially discourage renovations or new construction?
5. How much New Zealand bank lending is secured against residential and commercial land compared with productive business lending?
6. How much of recent New Zealand household wealth growth came from land rather than buildings?
7. Who would bear the initial loss if a national land-value tax were announced?
8. How should mortgage debt be treated?
9. Should the tax apply per parcel, per owner, or progressively by land value per square meter?
10. Should an ordinary owner-occupied home receive an exemption, deferral, or neither?
11. Would exemptions undermine the whole point by shifting the burden onto renters and commercial property?
12. How would deferred tax for cash-poor owners work at sale or death?
13. How should Māori collectively owned land be treated?
14. How should farms, conservation land, and land with low productive value be treated?
15. How much revenue could realistic rates raise?
16. Which taxes should be reduced in return?
17. How does Sweden currently tax land, buildings, housing gains, and rental property?
18. Could Sweden separate its municipal property fee more clearly into land and improvement components?
19. How does land-value taxation interact with the book’s argument about inheritance?
20. Would a land-value tax reduce housing prices, or mainly change who receives the value?

Final working summary

The argument is not:

Developers are good and landlords are bad.

It is not:

Introduce one tax and housing will be fixed.

It is:

Our tax system is much better at observing and taxing productive activity than it is at reclaiming the value society collectively creates in land. That creates a structural advantage for ownership over production.

And the line that should remain at the center of this material is:

We have spent decades taxing activity and rewarding scarcity. Then we wonder why our houses outperform our businesses.
