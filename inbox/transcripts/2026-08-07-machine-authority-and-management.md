Yes. There is already a substantial body of work around this, but your particular synthesis is interesting: **people may become attracted to machine authority not because machines are wonderful, but because their experience of human authority has become so poor.**

The closest intellectual thread I found is actually the intersection of workplace democracy, algorithmic management, disability/flexibility, and “augmented” democracy.

### The closest writers and researchers

- **Elizabeth Anderson — *Private Government*.** This is probably the philosophical foundation you want. Anderson argues that workplaces function as forms of “private government”: employees are subject to authorities that can order them around and sanction them, often with little accountability. She is not writing about AI, but she gives you the missing first half of your argument: *why escaping the human manager can feel like freedom*. 

- **Alex Rosenblat — *Uberland*.** Probably essential. Rosenblat studied Uber drivers and describes the platform as an automated or algorithmic boss. The important point is that eliminating the visible human manager did **not** eliminate managerial power; it embedded the rules in software, pricing, ratings, task allocation, nudges and information asymmetries. 

- **Jeremias Prassl — *Humans as a Service*.** He directly attacks the tension you identified: platforms promise entrepreneurship, autonomy and flexibility, while workers can simultaneously experience strict algorithmic surveillance and precariousness. His question is essentially how to preserve genuine flexibility without allowing “innovation” to erase basic employment protections. 

- **Antonio Aloisi & Valerio De Stefano — *Your Boss Is an Algorithm*.** This is the most literally relevant title. They look at AI, platform work and labor law as managerial functions move into algorithms. 

- **Phoebe V. Moore — *The Quantified Self in Precarity*.** Moore focuses on quantification, surveillance, metrics and the psychological consequences of being continuously measured. Her research connects workplace quantification with stress, anxiety and precarity. 

- **Colin Gavaghan, Alistair Knott & James Maclaurin — *The Impact of Artificial Intelligence on Jobs and Work in New Zealand*.** This is particularly valuable because it is explicitly New Zealand-focused. Their 2021 Otago report already makes a surprisingly close version of your argument: AI management could reduce favoritism and accommodate workers’ family, leisure and educational needs, but could equally isolate, dehumanize and surveil them. They explicitly warn against AI entrenching power disparities between workers, managers and capital owners. 

### There is empirical evidence for your “I’d rather deal with the machine” instinct

This part surprised me because somebody has tested something very close to your hypothesis.

Yoko Sugitani, Taku Togawa and Kosuke Motoki ran three experiments published in 2025. **Workers who had experienced social exclusion became more favorable toward algorithmic personnel assessment than human assessment**, partly because they perceived the algorithm as fairer. 

That's very close to:

> *I don't necessarily love the machine. I no longer trust the humans.*

Another 2025 experiment found something darker. Workers managed by an AI system tolerated substantially worse evaluations and lower pay without the same drop in motivation or perceived fairness seen under a human manager. The researchers argue that the muted emotional response to machine judgment could make algorithmic exploitation **easier**, not harder. 

That gives you a brilliant tension:

> **The machine may remove humiliation without removing exploitation.**

And research from HKUST involving more than 800 participants in China and the US found that people still generally preferred humans when empathy was important, while AI management was more acceptable for execution-focused tasks such as scheduling or routine requests. Visible human intervention when an AI made mistakes also increased trust. 

So the likely future isn't simply “AI boss replaces human boss.”

It's:

> **Use machines for consistency, patience, memory and routine coordination; retain humans for judgment, empathy and accountability.**

That feels very compatible with how you're thinking about organizations generally.

### Benji's experience has a research literature too

A 2025 qualitative study specifically examined gig work among people with disabilities. Participants identified **autonomy and flexible hours as real advantages**, while also reporting income insecurity, discrimination, lack of benefits and other accessibility barriers. 

That makes the Benji story potentially much more interesting than “Uber didn't work for my brother.”

It becomes:

> Platform work contains a genuinely emancipatory idea for people whose capacities don't fit a conventional nine-to-five schedule. But the execution can turn that freedom into precarity, inaccessible software or algorithmic control.

You don't therefore need to dismiss the gig model.

You can ask why we can't retain:

**“Work when your capacity allows”**

without also requiring:

**“Carry all the economic risk yourself.”**

That's a much more interesting question.

## The Swedish material is especially good

There is an active Swedish research program at Karolinska called **ALGOSH — Algorithmic Management at Work**. A recent Swedish study examined transport, retail and warehousing and found both improvements and deterioration in working conditions depending on what the technology did, how it was implemented, and the organizational context. Crucially for your union argument, workplaces with stronger local union organization reported more influence over the digital systems being introduced. 

Sweden also already gives you a concrete gig-economy case. Lund University researcher **Niklas Selberg** has written about Sweden’s first collective bargaining agreement for food-delivery riders and what it means for platform work, algorithmic management and the Swedish labor model. 

This supports a more nuanced version of your Swedish observation:

> **Sweden doesn't necessarily eliminate managerial power. It surrounds it with institutions capable of negotiating the terms under which that power is exercised.**

And then your criticism can remain: those institutions can themselves become remote, bureaucratic and difficult for individuals to identify with.

That's far more interesting than “unions good” or “unions bad.”

## And then there is AI government

Here I would slightly modify your claim.

There **is** evidence that the idea of replacing politicians with algorithms resonates with substantial numbers of people. An IE University survey in 2021 famously reported that more than half of Europeans surveyed supported replacing some MPs with algorithms. But later academic work points out an important problem: the actual question combined introducing AI with **reducing the number of politicians**, so some respondents may simply have been expressing their desire for fewer politicians. 

More rigorous recent work suggests people are considerably more cautious about being **governed by AI**. A German study found overall skepticism, especially when AI was given actual high-level decision authority rather than merely administrative or advisory roles. Interestingly, that study did **not** find strong evidence that political dissatisfaction itself explained enthusiasm for AI government. 

A 2026 UK study of more than 1,500 citizens found something I think is much more important for your book: **69.2% fell into groups preferring decisions to be shared between two or more actors—politicians, citizens, experts and/or AI—rather than dominated by one actor.** 

So I'd sharpen your observation to:

> **We haven't reached the point where people straightforwardly want machines to govern them. We've reached the point where giving machines a meaningful role in government no longer sounds absurd.**

That is itself an extraordinary change.

## César Hidalgo is almost exactly where you should go next

MIT researcher **César Hidalgo's “Augmented Democracy”** proposes giving individuals personalized AI representatives capable of learning their political preferences and predicting how they would vote on legislation. His goal isn't an all-knowing machine dictator; it's using AI to allow citizens to participate directly at a scale impossible under traditional representative democracy. He has a TED talk titled *A bold idea to replace politicians*. 

That should be on your immediate video queue.

Then look at **Audrey Tang and E. Glen Weyl's *Plurality***. Their approach is almost the opposite of “AI governor”: use technology to make **human collective intelligence** work better rather than replacing human pluralism with optimization. Their work draws heavily on Taiwan's experiments with digital democracy. 

And **Beth Simone Noveck's new 2026 book *Reboot: AI and the Race to Save Democracy*** is almost impossibly timely for what you're thinking about. Her thesis is that AI can be deliberately designed and governed to strengthen democratic institutions rather than simply automate them or destroy them. 

## I think your distinctive argument is here

Most of the literature divides into two camps.

One asks:

> **Will algorithmic management exploit workers?**

Another asks:

> **Can AI make management and government more efficient?**

Your question is psychologically different:

> **Why does machine authority sometimes feel attractive in the first place?**

And your answer is:

> Because human authority has become intolerable to a lot of people.

That gives you a progression:

**Bad human management**
→ workers value autonomy
→ platforms remove the visible manager  
→ algorithms quietly recreate managerial control  
→ workers gain some freedom but lose security and recourse  
→ generative AI introduces a genuinely different possibility: an infinitely patient, personalized helper  
→ the same idea starts becoming imaginable at organizational and even political scale.

But there is a warning running straight through it:

> **Removing the human manager doesn't remove power. It may merely make power harder to see.**

I think those two lines are worth preserving together:

> **If people would rather be managed by a machine than by another person, the interesting question isn't what happened to AI. It's what happened to management.**

And:

> **The machine may remove humiliation without removing exploitation.**

That feels like a serious new branch of **Shared Failure**: not economic inequality itself, but our declining confidence in the humans and institutions entrusted to exercise power over us.
