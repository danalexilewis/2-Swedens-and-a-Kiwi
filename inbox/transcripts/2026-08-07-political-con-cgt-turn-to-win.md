The political con we all fell for in nz

Yes. “I don’t want to lose my turn to win” is a very good description of part of the New Zealand psychology around capital gains tax. I would treat it as an interpretation rather than something we can prove from polling.

And there is an important correction to the premise: New Zealanders are no longer uniformly hostile to CGT. A 2025 RNZ-Reid Research poll found about 43% supported a tax on gains from investment property, 36% opposed and 22% were undecided. But support falls dramatically when the family home is included.  

That distinction tells us something.

The property ladder is a promise

For decades New Zealand has taught people a fairly simple wealth strategy:

Work → save deposit → buy house → endure mortgage → house appreciates → trade up → arrive at retirement with a valuable tax-advantaged asset.

Housing is New Zealand’s largest and most widely held form of household wealth, and Treasury itself describes the first home as the first rung on a wealth ladder that rising prices have increasingly pushed out of reach.  

Even the language is revealing:

“Get on the property ladder.”

University of Auckland housing research notes how pervasive that metaphor has become and how closely housing is associated with status and progress in New Zealand.  

So when you propose taxing capital gains, many people don’t hear:

“We’re going to make the tax system more neutral.”

They hear:

“You are changing the rules just before I get my turn.”

That’s the interesting psychological problem.

Imagine two generations

A 65-year-old bought a house for $180,000.

It eventually became worth $1.4 million.

They didn’t pay annual income tax on that $1.22 million increase simply because the house appreciated.

Then imagine their 32-year-old child.

They are looking at the same $1.4 million house thinking:

“Okay. My parents got rich through property. Housing is incredibly expensive because of that process. But eventually I’ll buy something too. And then I’ll get the same benefit.”

Now somebody proposes CGT.

The younger person can perversely think:

“Hang on. I already missed cheap houses. Now you’re going to tax the capital gain as well?”

That’s your turn-to-win problem.

They have been hurt by the system and can still become politically invested in preserving it because their imagined solution is eventually joining the winning side.

It resembles a queue

Imagine a casino with a ridiculous promotion.

Everyone who got inside before 1995 received enormous winnings.

The queue outside is now five kilometers long.

Instead of saying:

“This casino promotion is obviously broken.”

people in the queue say:

“Don’t cancel it. I’ve been waiting twenty years.”

That is the political genius of asset-based inequality.

It recruits some of the losers into defending the mechanism because they still hope to become winners.

That belongs in Shared Failure.

And the gains feel deserved

There is another psychological effect.

If somebody’s house rises from $500,000 to $1.5 million, they often don’t experience that million-dollar increase as government assistance.

They experience it as:

“My house.”

Maybe they:

* Worked hard to service the mortgage.
* Renovated it.
* Took interest-rate risk.
* Maintained it for thirty years.
* Sacrificed consumption to pay it off.

All true.

So when you propose taxing the gain, it feels like taxing their prudence.

But some of the gain may actually have come from:

* Falling interest rates
* Credit expansion
* Population growth
* Restricted land supply
* Infrastructure
* Schools
* Zoning decisions
* General wage growth
* Other people wanting to live nearby

That is exactly where your new Georgist perspective becomes useful.

You can respect the work someone put into their house while asking:

How much of this increase did you actually create?

The family home exemption exposes the contradiction

The 2019 Tax Working Group recognized this problem.

It recommended extending taxation of capital gains but excluded the main family home because that was outside its terms of reference. At the same time, it explicitly noted that excluding the family home creates an incentive to put more money into owner-occupied housing because both the housing service and eventual capital gain receive favorable treatment.  

Economically, that’s awkward.

Politically, it’s obvious.

The family home is almost sacred.

So governments end up saying:

Investment-property capital gains = income-like and taxable.

But:

My $2 million gain on my Auckland home = family security.

That isn’t a clean economic distinction.

It’s a political distinction.

There are legitimate objections to CGT too

You shouldn’t reduce every opponent to self-interest.

There are genuine design questions:

* Should inflationary gains be taxed?
* What happens when someone has a gain on paper but no cash?
* Do you tax only when they sell?
* Does that discourage selling?
* How are losses treated?
* What happens to founders whose companies increase enormously in value?
* What happens to farms?
* What about inherited assets?
* How do you value assets when the regime starts?
* Does it discourage productive investment?

Those are serious questions. New Zealand’s Tax Working Group spent hundreds of pages on exactly these design issues.  

And this gets back to why land tax may suit your argument better than a blanket CGT.

You don’t particularly want to tax someone for founding Eddy and selling it for $50 million in exactly the same way you tax someone whose Auckland section increased by $5 million because the city grew around it.

Those gains have different economic origins.

There’s also distrust of government

I think this is a substantial part of the politics.

If government says:

“We’re introducing another tax,”

people reasonably ask:

“What tax are you reducing?”

If the answer is none, somebody who already pays income tax and GST may see CGT as another claim on their savings rather than a correction of an imbalance.

That is why your Georgist formulation is politically stronger:

Don’t just tax capital more. Change what we tax.

For example:

More tax on:

* Scarce land
* Economic rents
* Very large inherited advantages

Less tax on:

* Wages
* Productive investment
* Building
* Entrepreneurship

Now you’re not saying:

Government deserves more money.

You’re saying:

The tax burden is sitting on the wrong things.

That’s much more compatible with your politics.

And there’s an even darker generational version

The old promise was:

Work hard and buy a house.

The emerging promise may be:

Work hard, rent, wait for your parents to die, inherit their property wealth, and finally acquire security.

That’s an extraordinary deterioration in the social contract.

And CGT politics can accidentally reinforce it.

A family thinks:

“Don’t tax Dad’s house. That’s how I’m eventually going to afford mine.”

So an adult child who has been excluded by housing inflation can end up defending the untaxed gains that caused much of their exclusion.

That gives you a very strong Two Swedens and a Kiwi formulation:

The system became so unequal that inheritance started looking like the solution to the inequality.

And underneath that sits your original observation:

Nobody wants to end the game because everyone is waiting for their turn to win.

I’d preserve both lines. The second is the emotional observation; the first is the economic conclusion.
