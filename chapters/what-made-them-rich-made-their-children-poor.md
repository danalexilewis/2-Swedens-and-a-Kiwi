## Opening scene

When I first moved to Sweden, my partner and I lived in an eighteen-square-metre flat in Guldheden. Poseidon owned the building. There was a toilet in the apartment. The shower was in another building. We had a mezzanine bed and a desk underneath it, and a huge window that opened onto the grass.

It was tiny. It was quiet. It felt far from the city even though it was close — a dead-end tram line will do that. Rent was low; later it was about fifteen hundred crowns a month. We moved into a bigger place in the same system: one bedroom, a lounge, two or three good years before we left.

I had dropped out of university in New Zealand. I arrived without a finished degree and without a plan to grind my way back into respectability. For the first time in my life, I felt free of having to study harder, work harder, prove harder.

Even though we were renting, those places felt like ours. Nobody was going to take them away.

That is the point. Not the romance of a small flat. Not nostalgia for being young. The point is security without ownership — a modest home that does not get pulled out from under you while you study, save, take a risk, or simply become an adult without living with your parents.

## Shared failure

What made older cohorts rich was not only work. Housing and related assets rose. Owners gained wealth and borrowing power. Those gains compounded and passed through families.

What made their children poor — poorer in the only sense that matters for adult membership — is that the same trajectory locked security behind ownership and inheritance, while the alternative path was left too small.

Sweden already had a version of that alternative: secure, modest, first-hand rental through companies like Poseidon — profit, more building, tenants who could plan a life. It did not build enough of it.

New Zealand barely has the idea. It has a private rental market that functions, in some ways, better than Sweden’s private secondary market. What it does not have is a widely available small-box security model that lets young people stand on something solid without parental capital.

Both countries protected asset values and short-term stability. Neither treated secure non-owned housing as infrastructure for the next generation at the scale the problem required.

## Swedish mechanism

Sweden’s housing story is not one market. First-hand tenure in the allmännytta / municipal-company world can feel like belonging. Private secondary renting is another country.

The Poseidon-type model is simple: secure rent; the company makes a profit; profit builds more apartments. The flats are not palaces. They do not need to be. They need to stay yours in the only way that matters when you do not own — they do not vanish because an amateur landlord changes their mind, or because an intermediary’s incentives point the wrong way.

Sweden’s failure here is not that the model is wrong. The failure is scarcity. Not enough of it. Queues and rationing turn a good institution into a lottery, and newcomers without the right contract fall into the private residual.

## New Zealand mechanism

New Zealand teaches ownership as the path to security. When prices outrun wages, that lesson becomes an inheritance lesson. Parental help decides who gets a foothold. Renting is widely treated as temporary failure on the way to a mortgage, not as a legitimate foundation for adult effort.

The country has tenancy law and dispute forums. The cost of landlord failure is still too low. Agents and owners get away with behaviour that would be rarer if failure hurt. That is a real problem.

It is not the deepest problem. The deepest problem is the missing institution: secure modest rental at scale — something Poseidon-like in function, not necessarily in name — so that youth can study, save, and take risk without waiting for the Bank of Mum and Dad.

## Shared outcome

Housing security becomes increasingly inherited. Work still pays for life. Capital decides whether you can build security. Countries that let ownership compound without building strong non-owned alternatives write off the independence of people without family balance sheets.

## Who benefited

Existing owners. Families who can transfer deposits or inherit. Political coalitions aligned with protecting asset values. Anyone for whom “housing policy” means protecting the price of what they already hold.

---

## Who carried the cost

People without family capital. Young renters in provisional arrangements. Anyone asked to study hard and take risks while standing on ice.

---

## Strongest counterargument

People still buy without inheritance by saving, moving to cheaper places, or accepting smaller homes. Sweden’s public rental queues show the model is scarce and contested. Tiny flats are not a national ideal. Ownership culture in New Zealand is a preference, not only a trap.

---

## Response

The claim is not that nobody without help ever succeeds. It is that family capital is becoming decisive for the security wages once bought — and that both countries under-built the alternative that makes success possible without that capital.

Eighteen square metres with a shower down the hall is not the dream. Knowing it will still be there next year is.

---

## Conclusion / proposal

New Zealand should copy the function of programmes like Poseidon: secure modest rental; provider earns a return; more homes get built; tenants can plan.

Sweden should expand what it already knows how to do. Having the model is not enough. Scale is the test.

*[proposal]*

---

## What must now be faced

If security is only for owners and heirs, the home-owning middle class dies as a broadly accessible class. If secure rental remains a scarce privilege, the same death arrives by another door.

Copy it. Expand it. Or keep pretending ownership alone will save the next generation.
